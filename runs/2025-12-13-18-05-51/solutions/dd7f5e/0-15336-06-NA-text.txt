That condition imposes a uniform 2-adic valuation condition. Let's check carefully.

Recall: For given d, the set M(d) = { m even : m | 2d and m ∤ d }.

Given d, what are the m in M(d)? Let's characterize. Let d be any integer ≥1. Write d = 2^{v} * t, where t odd. Then 2d = 2^{v+1} * t. Any even divisor m of 2d can be written as m = 2^{i} * s where 0 ≤ i ≤ v+1, s|t, s odd. Condition m ∤ d means that m does not divide d = 2^v * t. So for m dividing 2d, m will fail to divide d precisely when its 2-adic exponent i is greater than v (i.e., i = v+1). Because any divisor of 2d that does not have the maximal exponent of 2 (i ≤ v) divides d (since s|t). So the only even divisors of 2d that are not divisors of d are those with i = v+1 (i.e., having one extra factor of 2 beyond those dividing d). In other words, M(d) = { 2^{v+1} * s : s divides t }. That is, the set of m of the form 2^{v+1} * s where s|t (odd divisors of t). So M(d) consists of all even numbers with the same exact 2-adic valuation (v_2(m) = v+1) that divide 2d, and whose odd part divides t.

Thus any subset of M(d) can be realized with that same d. So the allowed sets of m correspond to subsets of some M(d), which are exactly sets of even numbers sharing the same v_2 exponent, and whose odd parts are divisors of t where t = d / 2^v.

Thus to be able to have product of any subset of even m's as factor of 1 + x^d, the set of m's must all share the same v_2 (i.e., same exponent of 2), because they must be of the form 2^{v+1} * odd_divisor.

Thus our earlier counting (all subsets) overcounts subsets with m having different 2-adic valuations. Those subsets cannot be simultaneously factors of a single 1 + x^d; i.e., they cannot be together divisors. However, maybe we could still realize them using different d?
