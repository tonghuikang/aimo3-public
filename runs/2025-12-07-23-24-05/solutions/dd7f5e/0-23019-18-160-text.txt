\tag{1}
\]

Thus \(A(x)\) must be a divisor (in \(\mathbb Z[x,x^{-1}]\)) of a binomial
\(x^{k}+x^{\ell}\).  After removing the monomial factor \(x^{k}\) we obtain

\[
x^{-k}A(x)\mid 1+x^{m}\qquad(m>0),\tag{2}
\]

so that, up to a monomial factor, \(A(x)\) is a divisor of some
\(1+x^{m}\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2.  Divisors of \(1+x^{m}\)

The factorisation of a binomial is well‑known:

\[
1+x^{m}= \prod_{\substack{d\mid 2m\\ d\not\mid m}}\Phi_{d}(x),
\]

where \(\Phi_{d}\) is the \(d\)‑th cyclotomic polynomial.
All indices \(d\) that appear satisfy **the same exact power of two**
\(v_{2}(d)=t\ge 1\); in fact \(d=2^{t}\,e\) with \(e\) odd, and the
exponent \(t\) is fixed by the chosen \(m\).

Consequently every divisor of a binomial \(1+x^{m}\) is a product of
distinct cyclotomic polynomials whose indices have the same 2‑adic
valuation.  Conversely, any such product does divide some \(1+x^{m}\)
(take \(m\) to be a common multiple of the odd parts).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  What \(\alpha\) can look like  

Write  

\[
A(x)=\pm x^{c}\;Q(x),\qquad 0\le c\le8,\;\deg A\le8,
\]

where  

\[
Q(x)=\prod_{d\in D}\Phi_{d}(x),\qquad
D\subseteq\{2,6,10,14,18,30\}\;(v_{2}=1)\\
\cup\{4,12,20\}\;(v_{2}=2)\\
\cup\{8,24\}\;(v_{2}=3)\\
\cup\{16\}\;(v_{2}=4),
\]

and the total degree \(\displaystyle\sum_{d\in D}\varphi(d)\) does not
exceed \(8-c\).  (The empty set \(D=\varnothing\) gives \(Q(x)=1\).)

Thus a shifty function is **exactly** a monomial times a product of
cyclotomic polynomials whose indices belong to a single 2‑adic group,
with the degree restriction above; the overall sign may be chosen
independently.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4.  Exhaustive enumeration  

The sets of admissible indices are

| \(v_{2}=1\) | \(v_{2}=2\) | \(v_{2}=3\) | \(v_{2}=4\) |
|------------|------------|------------|------------|
| \(\{2,6,10,14,18,30\}\) | \(\{4,12,20\}\) | \(\{8,24\}\) | \(\{16\}\) |

Only those subsets whose \(\varphi\)‑sum is \(\le 8\) are allowed.
A short program (see below) enumerates all admissible subsets, adds the
monomial factor \(x^{c}\) (with the degree bound) and finally counts the
two possible signs.

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.Symbol('x')

# cyclotomic polynomials with even index and φ ≤ 8
phi = {2:1,4:2,6:2,8:4,10:4,12:4,14:6,16:8,18:6,20:8,24:8,30:8}
phi_poly = {d: sp.cyclotomic_poly(d, x) for d in phi}

# group by 2‑adic valuation
groups = {1:[2,6,10,14,18,30],
          2:[4,12,20],
          3:[8,24],
          4:[16]}

def subsets(group):
    """all non‑empty subsets of a list of indices"""
    from itertools import combinations
    for r in range(1,len(group)+1):
        for comb in combinations(group,r):
            yield comb

polys = set()
# monomial only (empty product)
for c in range(9):
    polys.add( (c, sp.Integer(1)) )               # + sign
    polys.add( (c, sp.Integer(-1)) )              # – sign

# products inside a single 2‑adic group
for v, idxs in groups.items():
    for comb in subsets(idxs):
        # product of the chosen cyclotomics
        Q = sp.Integer(1)
        degQ = 0
        for d in comb:
            Q *= phi_poly[d]
            degQ += phi[d]
        for c in range(0,9-degQ):
            polys.add( (c,  Q) )
            polys.add( (c, -Q) )
print(len(polys))
```

The program prints **160**.  In other words, there are exactly

* 22 distinct monic polynomials \(Q(x)\) (including the constant 1);
* for each such polynomial, \(9-\deg Q\) admissible shifts \(c\);
* and the overall sign can be chosen arbitrarily.

Hence \(2\sum_{Q}(9-\deg Q)=160\) shifty functions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 5.  Why the answer \(266\) is too large  

Another solver counted **all** products of the 12 cyclotomic polynomials
listed above, *ignoring* the requirement that the indices share the same
2‑adic valuation.  There are 44 such monic products (including 1).

yields \(2·133 = 266\).
