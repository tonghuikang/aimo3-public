**Key insight**  
Write a shifty function \(\alpha\) as a polynomial  
\[
A(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{8}\alpha(i)x^{i}\in\mathbb Z[x],\qquad\deg A\le8 .
\]  
For a suitable \(\beta\) and distinct integers \(k\neq l\) we have  

\[
A(x)B(x)=x^{k}+x^{l}=x^{k}(1+x^{d}),\qquad d=|k-l|>0,
\]  

where \(B(x)=\sum_{j}\beta(j)x^{-j}\) is a Laurent polynomial.  
Thus \(A(x)=x^{a}f(x)\) with \(a\ge0\) and \(f(0)=\pm1\); cancelling the common factor \(x^{a}\) gives  

\[
f(x)\mid 1+x^{d}.
\]

**Cyclotomic factorisation**  
For any \(d\ge1\) write \(d=2^{r}m\) with \(m\) odd. Then  

\[
1+x^{d}= \prod_{\substack{e\mid 2d\\ e\nmid d}} \Phi_{e}(x)
      =\prod_{t\mid m}\Phi_{2^{\,r+1}t}(x),
\]  

so every irreducible factor of \(1+x^{d}\) is a cyclotomic polynomial \(\Phi_{e}\) whose index \(e\) has a **fixed 2‑adic valuation** \(v_{2}(e)=r+1\).  
Consequently any divisor \(f\) of a binomial \(1+x^{d}\) must be a product of cyclotomic polynomials all belonging to the **same \(v_{2}\)‑group**.

**Relevant cyclotomics (degree ≤ 8)**  

| \(v_{2}\) | cyclotomic \(\Phi_{e}\) | degree |
|---|---|---|
|1| \(\Phi_{2},\Phi_{6},\Phi_{10},\Phi_{14},\Phi_{18},\Phi_{30}\) | 1,2,4,6,6,8 |
|2| \(\Phi_{4},\Phi_{12},\Phi_{20}\) | 2,4,8 |
|3| \(\Phi_{8},\Phi_{24}\) | 4,8 |
|4| \(\Phi_{16}\) | 8 |

(Only even‑indexed cyclotomics appear because \(1+x^{d}\) has only even‑indexed factors.)

**Counting admissible \(f\)**  
Choose a single \(v_{2}\)‑group (or the empty product).  
For each subset of that group whose total degree \(s\le8\) we obtain a distinct primitive divisor \(f\).  
If \(f\) has degree \(s\), the monomial factor \(x^{a}\) can be any \(a\) with \(0\le a\le 8-s\); there are \(9-s\) choices.

Enumerating all subsets respecting the single‑group rule gives  

\[
\sum_{f}(9-\deg f)=80 .
\]

**Sign**  
Both \(f\) and \(-f\) work (multiply \(\beta\) by \(-1\)), so we double the count.

**Result**  

\[
\#\text{shifty functions}=2\times 80 = 160 .
\]

**Pitfalls to avoid**  
- Allowing products of cyclotomics from different \(v_{2}\) groups over‑counts; such a product never divides any \(1+x^{d}\).

**Key insight – divisor of a two‑spike pattern**  
For a shifty \(\alpha\) we need a \(\beta\) and distinct integers \(k,l\) such that  

\[
c(n)=S_n(\alpha)\star\beta=
\sum_{t}\alpha(t+n)\beta(t)=
\begin{cases}1&n=k,l\\0&\text{else}\end{cases}.
\]

Writing the generating polynomial \(A(x)=\sum_{i}\alpha(i)x^{i}\) and the reversed
\(B'(x)=\sum_{t}\beta(t)x^{-t}\), we have \(c(n)=\) coefficient of \(x^{n}\) in
\(A(x)B'(x)\). Hence we need  

\[
A(x)B'(x)=x^{k}+x^{l}=x^{m}(x^{d}+1),\qquad d=|k-l|,\;m=\min\{k,l\}.
\]

Since \(B'(x)\) may be a Laurent polynomial, the condition is exactly that
\(A(x)\) divides a sum of two monomials, i.e. \(A(x)=x^{s}f(x)\) where
\(f(x)\) divides \(x^{d}+1\) for some \(d>0\).  

**Cyclotomic description**  
\(x^{d}+1=\prod_{\substack{n\mid2d\\ n\nmid d}}\Phi_{n}(x)\).

\(r\) odd and \(t=v_{2}(d)\). Thus any divisor \(f\) is a product of a *subset*

\(x^{d}+1\) is square‑free.
